Landlord Won't Fix Repairs? Steps Renters Commonly Take

✍️ RenterAI Editorial Team 📅 May 2026 ⏱️ 8 min read 🇺🇸 All 50 States

The heat stopped working in January. You have sent three text messages, two emails, and knocked on your landlord's door twice. The response? Silence. Or worse — a vague promise that someone will "come by soon" that never leads to anything.

This situation is common. Many states and cities require landlords to keep rental homes in livable condition, and there are usually practical steps a renter can take to document the problem and ask for help.

Your Fundamental Right: A Habitable Home

Most states recognize what is called the "implied warranty of habitability," which generally means a landlord must keep a rental in livable condition regardless of what the lease says. The exact standards differ by state and by local housing code.

A habitable home means, at minimum:

  • Working heating and cooling systems
  • Working plumbing — hot and cold water, functioning toilets
  • Working electrical systems — no exposed wiring or fire hazards
  • Weatherproof roof, walls, windows, and doors
  • No serious mold, pest infestations, or structural hazards
  • Working smoke detectors and carbon monoxide alarms
  • Adequate security — functioning locks on doors and windows

These are examples of conditions that habitability rules and housing codes commonly cover. The exact requirements depend on your state and local code, so check the rules where you live.

What Repairs Is Your Landlord Responsible For?

There is often confusion about who is responsible for what. Here is the general rule:

Landlord's Responsibility

  • All structural repairs — roof, walls, foundation
  • Heating, plumbing, and electrical systems
  • Appliances provided with the unit (if included in the lease)
  • Common areas — hallways, laundry rooms, parking
  • Pest and rodent infestations
  • Mold caused by structural issues or water damage

Tenant's Responsibility

  • Damage you or your guests caused intentionally or through negligence
  • Minor maintenance — changing light bulbs, unclogging drains
  • Keeping the unit clean and sanitary
  • Repairs explicitly assigned to you in the lease (if legally permissible)

🚩 Watch out: Some leases try to shift landlord responsibilities onto tenants. Clauses that make tenants responsible for major repairs or "as-is" conditions are often unenforceable — a landlord cannot contract out of their duty to provide a habitable home.

Step-by-Step: What To Do When Your Landlord Won't Fix Things

Step 1 — Document the Problem Thoroughly

Before you do anything else, document the issue with photos and videos. Date-stamp everything. Write down when the problem started and how it is affecting your daily life. This documentation becomes critical evidence if the situation escalates to a formal complaint or legal action.

Step 2 — Submit a Written Repair Request

Even if you have complained verbally or by text, send a formal written notice via email or certified letter. State the problem clearly, the date it started, the impact on habitability, and a reasonable deadline for repair — typically 14 to 30 days for non-emergency issues, and immediately for emergencies like no heat in winter. Keep a copy of everything you send.

Step 3 — Follow Up in Writing

If the deadline passes with no action, send a follow-up letter stating that the repair remains unaddressed and that you are now considering your legal options. This creates a paper trail that shows you gave reasonable notice and opportunity to fix the problem.

Step 4 — Consider a Formal Letter

A formal letter is a more serious step than a text or email. It states the problem and the dates of your earlier requests, asks for the repair, and sets a reasonable deadline. Keep a copy. Our free AI tool can help you draft one for you to review and edit before sending.

Step 5 — File a Housing Code Complaint

Every city and county has a housing or building code enforcement office. File a complaint online or by phone. An inspector will visit the property and issue a formal notice to your landlord. This often prompts immediate action because landlords face fines and legal liability for code violations.

Step 6 — Talk to Legal Aid About Your Options

If the problem continues, a legal aid organization or attorney can explain which options your state offers and the risks of each. You can search for legal aid by state at LawHelp.org.

Options That May Exist, Depending on Your State

Below are general descriptions of options that exist in some places. We do not list state-by-state rules. Each option has strict requirements, many are not available in every state, and using one incorrectly can hurt your case or lead to an eviction case. Please talk to a legal aid organization or attorney before relying on any of them.

Housing Code Enforcement

A complaint to your local housing or building department can lead to an inspection and a written order for the landlord to fix violations. This is usually a lower-risk first step than anything involving your rent.

Rent Escrow, Repair-and-Deduct and Rent Withholding

Some states have laws that let a tenant pay rent into a court-supervised account, have a repair made and deduct the cost, or, in limited cases, withhold rent. Each has strict notice, timing and dollar rules, and many states do not allow them. Because a mistake can lead to an eviction case, get legal advice before taking any of these steps.

Rent Reduction (Rent Abatement)

In some places a court can reduce the rent to reflect the lower value of a unit with unresolved problems. A court decides whether this applies.

Damages and Ending the Lease

In some cases a tenant may be able to ask a court for damages for harm caused by an unresolved repair problem, or may have grounds to end a lease if a home is truly uninhabitable. These situations are fact-specific and carry risk, so ask a legal aid organization or attorney before moving out or stopping rent.

Emergency Repairs — When You Cannot Wait

Some repairs cannot wait two weeks for a formal process. If your heat fails in the middle of winter, a pipe bursts, or there is a serious security risk like a broken front door lock — here is what to do:

  1. Contact your landlord immediately by phone and follow up in writing the same day
  2. If you cannot reach them, contact your building manager or property management company
  3. For genuine emergencies affecting safety — gas leaks, flooding, no heat in freezing weather — call local housing code enforcement for an emergency inspection
  4. If safety is at risk, ask local housing code enforcement or a legal aid organization about your options, and keep records and receipts for any costs
