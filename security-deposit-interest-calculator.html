Do Landlords Pay Interest on Security Deposits?

It depends on where you live and what your lease says. Some states and cities require landlords to hold deposits in an interest-bearing account or to pay tenants interest, sometimes only for certain buildings or after a minimum time. Massachusetts is one example. In many places there is no interest requirement at all.

This calculator does not know your local rules. Enter the interest rate that applies to your situation, which may come from a law, your lease or the account where the deposit is held.

How the Interest Is Calculated

  • Simple interest: deposit × annual rate × years held.
  • Compounded annually: deposit × ((1 + rate) ^ years − 1).
  • Compounded monthly: deposit × ((1 + rate ÷ 12) ^ (12 × years) − 1).

Example: A $1,500 deposit held for 2 years at 1% simple interest earns $1,500 × 0.01 × 2 = $30.00.

Tips

  • Read your lease and ask your landlord where the deposit is held and whether interest is paid.
  • Keep a copy of your lease, your payment receipt and any account statements you receive.
  • Use our Security Deposit Checker and security deposit guide for how deposit returns work.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does my landlord have to pay interest on my security deposit?

It depends on your state, city and lease. Some places require interest or an interest-bearing account, often with conditions, and many do not. Check your local rules and your lease, or ask a legal aid organization.

What interest rate should I use?

Use the rate that applies to your situation, such as a rate set by law, a rate in your lease or the rate on the account holding the deposit. If you are unsure, ask your landlord for the rate in writing.

What is the difference between simple and compound interest?

Simple interest is calculated only on the original deposit. Compound interest is calculated on the deposit plus interest already earned, so it grows slightly faster over longer periods.

How is the time held calculated?

The calculator counts the days between the two dates and divides by 365 to get years.

Can the landlord keep the interest?

Some places allow the landlord to keep some or all of it, and others require it to go to the tenant. Rules vary, so check your local law and lease.

Is this tool legal advice?

No. It gives a general estimate based on the numbers you enter. Confirm what you are owed with your landlord or a legal aid organization.
