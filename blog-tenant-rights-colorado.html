Tenant Rights in Colorado 2026: Complete Renter's Guide

🔄 Corrected October 2026 ✍️ RenterAI Editorial Team 📅 June 2026 ⏱️ 12 min read 📍 Colorado — Denver, Boulder, Fort Collins 🆕 Major 2026 Law Changes

Colorado updated its security deposit rules on January 1, 2026. HB25-1249, "Tenant Security Deposit Protections," sets a default 30-day deadline to return deposits, defines "normal wear and tear," lets either side request a walk-through before move-out, and strengthens the penalties for wrongfully keeping a deposit. The state's cap on how much a landlord can require is a separate rule and is covered below.

Colorado has also passed cause-based eviction requirements (HB24-1098, 2024) and limits on late fees. If your understanding of Colorado tenant law is more than a year old, it is worth re-checking the rules that apply to you.

This guide covers the full picture — what changed in 2026, what has been in place since 2024, and what every Colorado renter should have memorized before signing or renewing a lease.

🆕 What HB25-1249 Changed (effective January 1, 2026)

According to the Colorado Division of Real Estate's official summary:

✅ Default deposit return deadline is 30 days (a lease may set a longer period, up to 60 days)
✅ "Normal wear and tear" has an updated legal definition
✅ Either the landlord or the tenant may request a walk-through before the lease ends (in person or by video)
✅ A landlord who does not follow the return rules forfeits the right to withhold any part of the deposit
✅ Wrongful retention can lead to treble (3x) damages if the tenant first gave written notice of the demand and intent to sue at least 7 days before filing
✅ Keeping 125% or more of the landlord's actual damages is presumed to be bad faith
✅ Fees for normal wear and tear, or for damage that existed before the tenancy, are void

Note: some websites report that HB25-1249 also lowered the deposit cap to one month and created a right to pay in installments. Those ideas appeared in earlier drafts of the bill, but the current text of C.R.S. § 38-12-102.5 still sets a two-month limit and the official summary does not list them. Check the current statute before relying on either.

⚖️ Colorado Law — C.R.S. Title 38

Colorado's landlord-tenant law sits primarily in C.R.S. Title 38. Key sections: C.R.S. 38-12-103 (security deposits), C.R.S. 38-12-503 (implied warranty of habitability), C.R.S. 38-12-301 (rent control preemption), C.R.S. 13-40-104 (eviction notice). HB24-1098 added cause-based eviction. HB25-1249 updated security deposit return rules effective January 1, 2026. Read the current statute text on the Colorado General Assembly website before relying on any detail here.

Colorado Security Deposit Rules — Including the 2026 Changes (HB25-1249)

HB25-1249 was signed on June 3, 2025 and took effect January 1, 2026. The Colorado Division of Real Estate publishes an official summary of the law. Here is what it says, explained practically.

How Much Can a Landlord Require?

Colorado limits a security deposit to two months' rent under C.R.S. § 38-12-102.5 (in effect since August 7, 2023). Some websites say HB25-1249 lowered this to one month and created a right to pay in installments. Those provisions were in earlier drafts of the bill, but they are not listed in the state's official summary of the enacted law, and the current statute text still says two months. If a landlord's request does not match what you read here, check the current statute or ask a legal aid organization.

The 30-Day Return Deadline

Colorado law previously said "one month." HB25-1249 changed the default to 30 days after the lease ends. A lease may provide a longer period, but not more than 60 days. The landlord must return the deposit, or send a written statement listing the exact reasons for any deductions, within that time.

"Normal Wear and Tear" Is Defined

The law defines normal wear and tear as deterioration, damage or uncleanliness that comes from the ordinary, intended use of the home without negligence, carelessness, accident or abuse. Uncleanliness that leaves the unit substantially less clean than when the lease began is not treated as normal wear and tear. Fees charged for normal wear and tear, or for problems that existed before you moved in, are void.

Walk-Through Before You Move Out

Either you or your landlord may request a walk-through before the lease ends, after you have had a chance to remove your furniture. It can happen in person or through a video call. Asking for one in writing creates a record of the unit's condition.

✅ Consider requesting a walk-through in writing. Send an email before your move-out date proposing a time. It creates a paper trail and gives both sides a chance to talk through any issues while you are still there.

If the Landlord Doesn't Follow the Rules

A landlord who does not comply with the return rules, or who wrongfully keeps a deposit, loses the right to withhold any part of it. Wrongful retention can lead to treble (three times) damages, provided you told the landlord in writing that you demand the deposit and intend to file a legal action at least seven days before filing. In a case, the landlord has the burden of showing the withholding was not wrongful.

The law also treats retaining 125% or more of the landlord's actual damages as presumptively "unreasonable," and it defines bad faith to include keeping a deposit without actual cause or for a retaliatory or discriminatory purpose.

If a property is sold or the landlord's interest ends during your tenancy, the landlord has 60 days to transfer the deposit to the new owner (and notify you by mail) or return it to you.

🚩 Deposit not returned on time? Review the deadline in your lease, then consider sending a written demand for the deposit and an itemized statement. If the landlord still does not respond, a legal aid organization or a licensed attorney can explain your options, including whether treble damages may apply to your situation.

Cause-Based Eviction — HB24-1098

This one has been in effect since 2024, but many Colorado renters still do not know about it. Under HB24-1098, landlords must have a legally valid reason to evict a tenant or refuse to renew a lease once a tenant has established residency. This is called cause-based or just-cause eviction.

Valid causes for eviction or non-renewal include non-payment of rent, serious lease violations, the owner intending to move in, demolition or major renovation, or the landlord taking the unit off the rental market. What landlords cannot do is refuse to renew your lease simply because they want to find a new tenant willing to pay higher rent. That practice — sometimes called "renoviction" — is now a legally prohibited basis for non-renewal in Colorado.

Eviction Notice Periods

For non-payment of rent, your landlord must give you 10 days written notice to pay or vacate. For lease violations, the notice period depends on the severity. For non-renewal of a lease without cause, the notice required depends on how long you have lived there:

  • Less than 1 month of tenancy: 7 days notice
  • 1 to 12 months of tenancy: 21 days notice
  • 1 year or more of tenancy: 91 days notice

These are substantial notice periods — especially the 91-day requirement for long-term tenants, which gives you almost three months to find new housing when a landlord exercises a valid cause for non-renewal.

🚩 Self-Help Eviction is Illegal. Colorado landlords cannot change your locks, remove your belongings, shut off utilities, or physically remove you without a court order. Doing so violates C.R.S. 38-12-510 and entitles you to remain in possession, actual damages, and potentially punitive damages. If this happens, call the police and contact Colorado Legal Services immediately.

Late Fee Cap

Colorado caps late fees at the greater of $50 or 5% of the monthly rent. If your rent is $1,500, the maximum late fee is $75. If your rent is $800, the maximum is $50. A late fee above this amount may not be enforceable, and you may be able to dispute the excess; check the statute and your lease or ask a legal aid organization.

Right to a Habitable Home

Colorado's implied warranty of habitability under C.R.S. 38-12-503 requires landlords to maintain your rental in a condition fit for human habitation throughout the entire tenancy. This includes working heat, working plumbing with hot water, structural soundness, working electrical systems, and freedom from serious pest infestations.

If your landlord fails to make required repairs after written notice, Colorado gives you several remedies: you can repair and deduct the cost from rent, withhold rent until repairs are made, or terminate the lease if conditions are severe enough. If you are a tenant receiving certain government benefits, landlords with more than five units may be required to attempt mediation before proceeding with eviction — at no cost to you.

No Rent Control in Colorado

Colorado prohibits cities and counties from enacting rent control on private residential property under C.R.S. 38-12-301. Landlords can raise rent by any amount. However, rent can only be increased once per 12-month period — Boulder has limited mobile home park rent stabilization, but this does not apply to standard apartments or houses.

Frequently Asked Questions

What changed in Colorado tenant law in 2026?+

HB25-1249 (effective January 1, 2026) updated Colorado's security deposit return rules: a 30-day default return deadline (up to 60 days if the lease says so), a defined meaning of normal wear and tear, the option for either side to request a walk-through, loss of the right to withhold if the landlord does not follow the rules, and treble damages after written notice for wrongful retention. Confirm details in the current statute.

Can I pay my Colorado security deposit in installments?+

Colorado law has not been confirmed to give a statewide right to pay a security deposit in installments. Some websites say HB25-1249 created one, but the official summary of the enacted law does not list it. If you need a payment plan, ask the landlord and get the arrangement in writing, and check the current statute text.

How long does a landlord have to return my deposit in Colorado?+

The default is 30 days after the lease ends (up to 60 days if your lease says so). The landlord must return the deposit or send a written statement of the exact reasons for any deductions in that time. If a landlord wrongfully keeps a deposit, treble damages may be available after written notice at least 7 days before filing.

Does Colorado require cause to evict a tenant?+

Yes — under HB24-1098, landlords must have a legally valid reason to evict or refuse to renew a lease. Valid causes include non-payment, lease violations, owner move-in, or demolition. Landlords cannot refuse to renew simply to find a higher-paying tenant. Non-renewal notice periods: 7 days (under 1 month tenancy), 21 days (1-12 months), 91 days (1+ year). For non-payment eviction: 10-day notice to pay or vacate.

What is the late fee cap in Colorado?+

The greater of $50 or 5% of monthly rent. Example: $1,500 rent = maximum $75 late fee. $800 rent = maximum $50. Any amount above this in your lease is unenforceable. Late fees must be specified in your lease — they cannot be charged without a written provision. This cap applies statewide to all residential leases.

Is there rent control in Colorado?+

No statewide rent control — C.R.S. 38-12-301 prohibits cities and counties from enacting it on private residential property. Landlords can generally raise rent, but proper notice is required; check your lease and current Colorado law for the notice rules that apply to you. Boulder has limited mobile home park stabilization only. Denver has no rent control for standard rentals.

Can my landlord enter my Colorado apartment without notice?+

Colorado does not have a specific statute requiring advance notice for general entry — your lease controls this. Many leases require 24 hours notice. For bed bug inspections specifically, 48 hours written or electronic notice is required by statute (C.R.S. 38-12-1004). Only genuine emergencies allow entry without any notice. Check your lease for the entry notice clause and hold your landlord to it.
